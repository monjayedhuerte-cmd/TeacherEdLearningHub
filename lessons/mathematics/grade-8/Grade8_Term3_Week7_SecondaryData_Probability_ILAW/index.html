Motivation — Data Detective

Look at a graph in a newspaper, school report, weather report, or website. Ask: Who collected the data? and Why was it collected?

I — LEARN⏱️ 10 min

What is Secondary Data?

Secondary data is data that has already been collected by another person, group, agency, or organization and is then used for a new analysis.

Primary data

You collect it yourself for your investigation.

Secondary data

You use data already collected by another source.

Graph-reading checklist

  1. Read the title.
  2. Identify the source/context.
  3. Check the categories and labels.
  4. Read the scale and units.
  5. Compare values only after checking the scale.
Example: A school uses a government report showing annual rainfall in a city. The school did not collect the rainfall measurements, so the report is secondary data for the school's analysis.

Think Like an Analyst

A graph shows that Thursday has 36 library visits and Monday has 18. A student says, “Thursday is exactly twice as busy as Monday.” Is the statement supported by the displayed values?

Yes. 36 ÷ 18 = 2, so the Thursday value is twice the Monday value. The conclusion is supported by the numbers shown in the graph.

Interpret & Analyze Secondary Graphs

📅 Date: __________⏱️ 50 minutes

⏱️ 5 min

Motivation — Which Claim Is Stronger?

Claim A: “Category B is bigger.” Claim B: “Category B is 12 units higher than Category A.” Which claim gives more evidence?

I — LEARN⏱️ 10 min

Four Moves for Analysis

1. Describe

State what the graph shows.

2. Compare

Look for greater, less, equal, or differences.

3. Identify patterns

Notice increases, decreases, clusters, or unusual values.

4. Conclude

Answer the question using evidence.

Worked Example

A published report gives weekly screen-time totals: Mon 2 h, Tue 3 h, Wed 4 h, Thu 3 h, Fri 5 h.

Describe: Friday is highest at 5 h. Compare: Friday is 3 h higher than Monday. Pattern: Values generally rise, with a drop on Thursday. Conclude: The highest recorded day is Friday.

DAY 3 • WEDNESDAY

Theoretical vs Experimental Probability

📅 Date: __________⏱️ 50 minutes

Daily Target: I can distinguish theoretical probability from experimental probability and explain how they are related.

⏱️ 5 min

Motivation — Before We Toss

For a fair six-sided die, what probability do you expect for rolling a 4?

I — LEARN⏱️ 10 min

Two Ways to Talk About Chance

Theoretical Probability

What should happen based on equally likely possible outcomes.

P(E) = favorable outcomes ÷ total possible outcomes

For a fair die, P(4) = 1 ÷ 6.

Experimental Probability

What happened in an actual experiment.

P(E) = number of favorable trials ÷ total trials

If 50 rolls produce twelve 4s, experimental P(4) = 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24.

As the number of trials becomes large, experimental results often move closer to the theoretical probability, but a short experiment may differ.
L — EXPLORE⏱️ 10 min

Predict, Then Observe

Suppose a coin is tossed 20 times. Theoretical probability of heads is 1/2. Which result is possible?

A — APPLY / PRACTICE-PLAY⏱️ 15 min

W — THINK / CHECK / MASTER⏱️ 10 min

Why Aren't They Always Equal?

Explain why an experimental probability of 0.45 for heads does not mean the theoretical probability of a fair coin is 0.45.

Theoretical probability comes from the possible outcomes of a fair coin: 1/2 = 0.50. Experimental probability comes from one set of observed trials, which can vary because of chance.

DAY 4 • THURSDAY

Run a Probability Investigation

📅 Date: __________⏱️ 50 minutes

Daily Target: I can conduct a simple probability experiment, record outcomes, calculate experimental probability, and compare it with a theoretical value.

⏱️ 5 min

Motivation — Make a Prediction

For a fair coin, predict the number of heads in 30 tosses. Your prediction is a hypothesis, not a guarantee.

I — LEARN⏱️ 10 min

How to Conduct the Investigation

  1. State the experiment and theoretical probability.
  2. Set a fixed number of trials.
  3. Record each outcome carefully.
  4. Count favorable outcomes.
  5. Calculate experimental probability.
  6. Compare the experimental and theoretical values.
  7. Write a conclusion using evidence.
Experimental P(E) = favorable outcomes ÷ total trials

Investigation Lab

Scenario A

A spinner has 4 equal sections: red, blue, green, yellow. In 40 spins, blue appears 13 times.

Scenario B

A fair die is rolled 60 times. A 2 appears 8 times. Theoretical P(2) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167.

W — THINK / CHECK / MASTER⏱️ 10 min

Compare & Explain

In the die scenario, the experimental value is lower than the theoretical value. Does that prove the die is unfair?

No. One experiment can differ from the theoretical probability simply because of random variation. More trials and additional evidence would be needed before making a stronger claim.

What We Learned This Week

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Ignoring the graph's title, source, labels, or scale.
  • Confusing theoretical probability with what happened in one experiment.
  • Writing a conclusion without citing evidence.
  • Assuming a small difference automatically proves an experiment is unfair.

Final reflection: “The most important evidence I should use when making a conclusion is ______.”
