Lesson 1 — Estimating Differences

Estimate differences by rounding minuends and subtrahends.

Target from the lesson
Estimating differences by rounding minuends and subtrahends.

Source context: the lesson introduces the Philippine Deep / Mindanao Trench and uses a measurement situation to connect estimation with real-world quantities.

I — Learn

What is a difference?

A difference is the number that results when one number is subtracted from another.

The minuend is the number from which another number is subtracted. The subtrahend is the number being subtracted.

minuend − subtrahend = difference
I — Learn

What does estimating mean?

An estimate is a close value that is easier to calculate. For a difference, round both numbers to the place named in the problem, then subtract.

Remember: The goal is not the exact difference. The goal is a reasonable value that is quick to find.
E — Explore

Worked Example: Round to the nearest hundred

Estimate: 7,846 − 3,218
7,846 → 7,800 and 3,218 → 3,200
7,800 − 3,200 = 4,600
Estimated difference: 4,600
  1. 1️⃣ Identify the requested place: hundreds.
  2. 2️⃣ Round the minuend.
  3. 3️⃣ Round the subtrahend.
  4. 4️⃣ Subtract the rounded numbers.
  5. 5️⃣ Ask: “Does the estimate make sense?”

Why do we round both numbers?
Because the difference is based on both numbers. Rounding both the minuend and subtrahend gives a quick, consistent estimate.

Lesson 2 — Subtracting Numbers without Regrouping

Subtract 4- to 6-digit numbers without regrouping.

Target from the lesson
Subtracting 4- to 6-digit numbers without regrouping.

The source practice begins with simple patterns such as 8 − 5, 80 − 50, and 800 − 500 to reinforce place-value structure.

I — Learn

When is regrouping NOT needed?

Regrouping is unnecessary when every digit in the minuend is large enough to subtract the corresponding digit in the subtrahend.

Place-value rule: Ones go under ones, tens under tens, hundreds under hundreds, and so on.
E — Explore

Worked Example

68,745 − 23,412 = 45,333

Ones: 5 − 2 = 3
Tens: 4 − 1 = 3
Hundreds: 7 − 4 = 3
Thousands: 8 − 3 = 5
Ten-thousands: 6 − 2 = 4

  1. 1️⃣ Align the place values.
  2. 2️⃣ Start with the ones place.
  3. 3️⃣ Subtract from right to left.
  4. 4️⃣ Record each difference in its correct column.
  5. 5️⃣ Check by adding the difference to the subtrahend.
Place-Value Pattern
Expression Answer What stays the same?
8 − 5 3 3 ones
80 − 50 30 3 tens
800 − 500 300 3 hundreds
8,000 − 5,000 3,000 3 thousands
P — Practice-Play
🎮 Solve: 8,765 − 2,341.
T — Think
Why must the digits be aligned?
Each position has a different value. Alignment makes sure ones are subtracted from ones, tens from tens, hundreds from hundreds, and so on.
C — Check
True or false: If every top digit is equal to or greater than the bottom digit, regrouping is not needed.
M — Master Challenge
Solve: 654,321 − 123,210.
531,111. No regrouping is needed because each digit in the minuend is sufficient for its corresponding subtraction.

Lesson 3 — Subtracting Numbers with Regrouping

Subtract 4- to 6-digit numbers when regrouping is needed.

Target from the lesson
Subtracting 4- to 6-digit numbers with regrouping.

The source lesson connects subtraction to the Puerto Princesa Underground River and explains a real-world measurement context.

I — Learn

What is regrouping?

Regrouping means exchanging one unit from a higher place for 10 units of the next lower place. For example, 1 ten can become 10 ones.

Trigger: If the top digit is smaller than the digit below it, you need to regroup before subtracting that column.
E — Explore

Step-by-Step Example: 6,432 − 2,785

  1. 🔹 Ones: 2 cannot subtract 5. Regroup 1 ten as 10 ones: 12 − 5 = 7.
  2. 🔹 Tens: after giving away one ten, 2 tens remain. 2 cannot subtract 8. Regroup 1 hundred as 10 tens: 12 − 8 = 4.
  3. 🔹 Hundreds: after regrouping, 3 hundreds became 2 hundreds. 2 cannot subtract 7. Regroup 1 thousand: 12 − 7 = 5.
  4. 🔹 Thousands: 5 − 2 = 3.
6,432 − 2,785 = 3,647
Common mistake: Do not forget to reduce the higher place after you regroup from it.
P — Practice-Play
🧠 Solve: 7,205 − 3,468.
P — Practice-Play 2
Which exchange is correct?
T — Think
Why does regrouping not change the total value?
It only changes the form of the same value. For example, 1 ten and 10 ones both represent 10.
C — Check
Self-check: Did I start at the ones? Did I reduce the place I regrouped from? Did I continue left one place at a time?
M — Master Challenge
Solve: 54,302 − 27,846.
26,456. Regroup as needed from right to left, carefully carrying each change into the next column.

Lesson 4 — Subtracting with Zeros in the Minuend

Regroup when the minuend contains zeros.

Target from the lesson
Subtracting from a number that has zeros as digits.

The source page explicitly explores different ways of regrouping 10,000 before subtracting from numbers with zeros.

I — Learn

Why are zeros tricky?

A zero in the minuend has no units available in that place. If subtraction requires units there, regrouping must travel from a higher place that has value.

Key idea: Regrouping can move across several zero places. Each exchange creates 10 units in the next lower place.
E — Explore

Different forms of 10,000

Equivalent form Value
10 thousands 10,000
9 thousands + 10 hundreds 10,000
9 thousands + 9 hundreds + 10 tens 10,000
100 hundreds 10,000
1,000 tens 10,000
10,000 ones 10,000

These are different ways to name the same value. This idea makes subtraction across zeros possible.

P — Practice-Play
Which is equal to 10,000?
P — Practice-Play 2
10,000 − 6,785 = ?
3,215. Regroup across the zeros so the lower places receive the units needed for subtraction.
T — Think
Why can a zero place become a nonzero digit after regrouping?
Regrouping changes how the same total value is distributed among place values. The zero place receives units from the higher place through the exchange.
C — Check
Self-check: When I see zeros, I look left for a place that can be regrouped, then pass the value through the zero places carefully.
M — Master Challenge
Solve: 50,000 − 27,864.
22,136. Regroup from the higher place and carefully adjust each place value as the exchange moves toward the ones place.

Lesson 5 — Properties of Addition

Use addition properties to make calculations easier.

Target from the lesson
Using the properties of addition to make calculations easier.

The source page defines the commutative, associative, and identity properties and introduces compatible numbers.

I — Learn

Commutative Property

a + b = b + a

The order of addends can change without changing the sum.

45 + 59 = 59 + 45
I — Learn

Associative Property

(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)

The grouping of addends can change without changing the sum.

(20 + 30) + 7 = 20 + (30 + 7)
I — Learn

Identity Property

a + 0 = a

Adding zero keeps the number the same.

83 + 0 = 83
I — Learn

Compatible Numbers

Compatible numbers are numbers whose sum is easy to calculate.

49 + 51 = 100
E — Explore

Make a difficult addition easier

Suppose we have 59 + 45 + 41. Notice that 59 and 41 are compatible because they make 100.

(59 + 41) + 45 = 100 + 45 = 145

The associative property lets us choose the useful grouping, while compatible numbers make that group easy to add.

Use a smart grouping to solve 59 + 45 + 41.

C — Check
Remember: Commutative = order; Associative = grouping; Identity = add 0; Compatible = easy-to-add pair.
M — Master Challenge

Commutative Property of Addition. The addends changed order, but the sum stayed the same.

Lesson 6 — Mental Addition

Apply techniques that make addition easier to calculate mentally.

Target from the lesson
Applying the techniques in mental addition.

The source activity asks learners to form an equation with a sum of 99 using six addition signs and the digits 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, and 1, with no digit repeated as an addend.

I — Learn

What is mental addition?

Mental addition means calculating without writing every step on paper. The goal is to notice useful number relationships and choose an efficient strategy.

Make a friendly number: adjust a number toward 10, 100, or another easy value.
Use compatible numbers: pair numbers that make an easy total.
Break apart numbers: separate tens and ones or other place values.
Reorder and regroup: use addition properties to create helpful pairs.
E — Explore

Strategy Example: Make a friendly number

38 + 27
Take 2 from 27 and add it to 38: 40 + 25 = 65.

The total stays the same because the 2 was moved from one addend to the other.

98 + 37
Think 100 + 37 − 2 = 135.

Why can making a friendly number make mental addition faster?
Numbers such as 10, 20, 50, and 100 are easy to combine mentally. A small adjustment can turn a difficult addend into a friendlier one.
